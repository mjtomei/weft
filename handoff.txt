Weft — Learning to organize computation

Project handoff

Part I — Introduction: copies are the native affordance

Humans have a single continuing existence and cannot trivially copy themselves into simultaneous continuations. That pattern casts a shadow over how we design agents. A digital agent, by contrast, can copy their working state and pursue several continuations at once. The resulting tree of existences is beyond human comprehension, much like the models we have trained, but it is the path toward better performance and lower cost. Many previous works have identified and started breaking down this barrier. Best-of-N evaluation shows the potential benefit, but systems attempting to exploit the technique keep the tree understandable by controlling it by hand, and no work has delivered a fully transparent and trainable loom for arbitrary graphs of machine states. This project takes the bitter lesson instead: learn the organization of the tree rather than prescribe it.

Relative to training agents to generate and run massively parallel tasks themselves, copying lowers the burden of asking for help. Invoking a specialized subagent or predefined workflow ordinarily asks the agent to recognize both that they need help and what kind of help to request. A fork can require only the judgment that another continuation could be useful. The copy inherits the situation and can discover what work is needed. A detailed task decomposition or specialist brief is optional. This also preserves information whose importance the parent has not yet recognized and therefore might omit from a delegation brief.

Instead of the prescribed parallelism used today, this work builds a loom that includes the state of the computer itself: the session and the executable workspace branch together. A copy can act on the files, artifacts, and tool results accumulated so far without reconstructing the situation from a description. That continuity is the basis for transparency to the agent and for making organization an ordinary, trainable part of execution.

The economic case rests on established results. Mixture of experts showed that frontier capabilities do not depend on all the frontier weights operating continuously and with dense connection. Subagents and summaries showed that the average context can stay small without hurting performance. Prescribed best-of-N showed higher performance at lower cost, and parallel work shortens a task's latency while maintaining its performance, which lets existing hardware run at higher-profit operating points without harming the project's critical path.

Each is a prescription: someone decides in advance when to branch, what to delegate, and how to combine the results. Improving a prescription takes specialized training, gains in the base model's general intelligence reach it only partially, and none of it is transparent to the user. Writing a prescription is also harder than noticing that help is needed. A fork needs only that judgment, so it is generic across tasks. Generic means trainable: there is one behavior to learn, ordinary training pipelines can target it directly, and every gain in the base model carries over to it. Generic also means it can stay on all the time, on every task, as ordinary behavior of the model rather than a specialized mode. That is what transparent and trainable mean here, and it is what lets the value reach consumer use. Selection, communication, and integration are part of that latency and cost. They do not have to be cheap; they have to be visible, priced, and trainable.

The same primitive extends from identical copies to transparent model specialization (§12.1): one base model controls the root session while forks can target models differentiated by prompting or weights. We have already seen that decreasing the number of active weights does not prohibit frontier performance. Token-level MoE routes every token across experts and requires tight, high-bandwidth interconnects; routing at `fork` shifts communication to branch boundaries and compact emissions, so specialized models on separate modest machines can do substantial local work over the inherited session and workspace before reporting back, without prescribed specialist roles or lossy delegation briefs.

Taking the bitter lesson seriously means exposing a generic fork/join primitive and a budget, and learning the organization policy from outcomes. Internally, the swarm determines when to fork, where, to which model or specialization, how wide, how much context to inherit, what each branch should do, what and when to communicate, what evidence to use, which results to incorporate, and when to stop; the number of branches, N, emerges from execution within a resource budget. The swarm also owns selection, integration, and final delivery, and chooses whether to interrupt a peer or consult a quarantined copy of the peer (§5). Every one of these decisions is the model's, so the paradigm grows more capable and efficient as the models do.

The bitter lesson bears on hardware as well. The most capable models are being built to target hardware that is extremely expensive and not widely available: large nodes with tight interconnects. If scaling is the priority, the target cannot be one machine scaled up indefinitely. It has to be models small enough to run on devices that are distributed geographically and in every other way devices can be distributed, which means consumer-level hardware in very large swarms. The same argument was made in systems research when large shared-memory multiprocessors were the frontier: on economics alone, clusters of commodity machines won, and high-performance computing then built its supercomputers from commodity parts (§14.6). None of this implies that the collapse in the cost of programming leaves consumer hardware untouched: volume is what carries over, and the shape of that compute is likely to change now that humans are not the ones programming it (§13).

Mixture of experts was one step toward this kind of thinking: capability grows with total parameters while only a few experts are active per token. Its routing still happens at every token and layer, though, so the experts must share one tightly coupled node. Routing at `fork` moves the same idea onto loosely coupled hardware: the fork primitive, compact emissions, and model specialization (§12.1) are how this project fits that shape, and in that respect it is downstream of the earlier work in the Omerta repositories (§13).

1. The product gap: best-of-N is not a primitive

Best-of-N supplies the motivation for expecting a small initial learning signal: where additional attempts help, there should be useful organization to capture. The first engineering target is to obtain the benefit of multiple attempts while spending less than a naive fixed-N implementation; savings could come from sharing work before forks, exploring only meaningful alternatives, reusing discoveries, and stopping unproductive work. The empirical question is how far learning can extend that benefit. Begin with ordinary tasks that benefit from multiple attempts; specially constructed decomposition tasks are not a prerequisite. The main result is the cost-performance Pareto frontier, including dollar cost and task latency, with outcome-labeled traces available for further learning.

Today, best-of-N with a day-to-day agent requires explicit orchestration. The user or harness must ask for multiple attempts, define N, isolate the environments, collect the results, and select. The available forms are all manual:
- Prompt the model to try several approaches. They serialize them, explore superficially, and have no isolated environments.
- Spawn N subagents or parallel sessions. The user defines N and the task split; the workspace is shared or manually worktreed; selection is manual.
- Use an API n parameter. That samples tokens, not tool-using sessions, and provides no environment isolation.
- Build a fixed-N pipeline. N and cost are fixed, with no adaptivity and no reuse across attempts.

What is missing is not the algorithm; best-of-N is well understood. What is missing is the packaging: one request in, internal exploration, selection by execution feedback, and one result out, with the user never managing branches. The transparent version requires:
- A cheap environment fork, so an attempt does not replay setup.
- A fork tool the model can see and learn to use, rather than an orchestration the user writes.
- Evidence for selection: tests, validators, or executable checks where available; gathering that evidence is part of the swarm's job.
- Budget control and a visible cost line, because transparency to the user must not mean invisibility of the bill.
- Cleanup and provenance, so the user gets one workspace and the runtime keeps the loom.

This is the product thesis, and it is distinct from "a smarter model": the same model gets multiple attempts without the user managing them. The swarm decides when to fork, what to communicate, which results to incorporate, and how to deliver the final result; the runtime isolates execution, accounts for resources, and handles cleanup; the user sees one result.

User-visible capability is a property of the system, not the weights. A transparent runtime that explores internally is indistinguishable, at the interface, from a smarter model: same request, same output shape, better answer. That is the intended product claim. The scientific claim is separate: the weights are unchanged, and any gain comes from organization at matched or lower cost. Keep the two claims distinct so neither borrows the other's evidence. The product claim is only honest if the interface really is unchanged and the cost is disclosed to the operator, even when it is hidden from the user.

1.1 Evidence status

Most workspace-fork tools report only systems metrics, with one recent exception that pairs container/process checkpointing with an external search controller:
- BranchFS: 292-317 microseconds branch creation, 317 microseconds to 2.1 ms commit, 1.66 GB/s FUSE read (7.24 GB/s with passthrough). No task accuracy.
- LayerFS: filesystem benchmarks (1.71x faster than the comparison image on fs-bench, 19.9x smaller than btrfs + zstd on a 157-snapshot history, zero unexpected pjdfstest failures). No agent accuracy.
- simgit: 10 MiB versus 567 MiB per microsoft/vscode worktree; eight worktrees in 6.4-7.0 s versus 14.0-14.2 s. No agent accuracy.
- StateFork / Waypoint (arXiv 2609.38648, Sept 2026): evaluates branch-based exploration over Waypoint snapshots (OverlayFS + CRIU + persistent PTY sessions) on Terminal-Bench, matching pass@20 in under 10 minutes and beating alternative execution substrates by 26% task accuracy and up to 70% faster exploration. Its exploration policy is an external tree-search controller outside the agent, not a model-owned primitive.
- BranchContext: exploration patterns and filesystem numbers. No agent accuracy.

The rest of the accuracy evidence is at the strategy level, without workspace forking. EnCompass (NeurIPS 2025) reports an ARC subset rising from 4.3% for a two-step agent to 11.7% with best-of-8 and 16.0% with best-of-36 (GPT-3.5), and a Reflexion agent moving from 35.5% to 36.1% with best-first search at lower cost per task. Best-of-N, self-consistency, Tree of Thoughts, Reflexion, and beam search all have published gains from sampling and selection.

The fork tool is an efficiency and state-continuity enabler: it can improve accuracy at a fixed budget by making exploration over live machine state cheaper, and it can improve latency by reusing setup. Putting that primitive inside the swarm's own action space is what remains to test.

2. Terminology: the elastic harness and the loom

An elastic harness is an agent harness that allocates inference-time compute dynamically: it branches the agent's execution — session, tools, environment, and model calls — when an alternative is worth exploring, joins results when evidence arrives, and stops when the budget or the evidence says to. The user sees one request and one result; the harness absorbs the branching. This is the name. "Dynamic branching" describes the mechanism, "transparent best-of-N" describes the benefit for users who know the baseline, and "adaptive inference-time scaling" is the academic umbrella. The distinction from session branching (conversation only) and server-side search (reasoning only) is that the elastic harness branches the execution across both sides. Best-of-N fixes five things — the fork point (at the start), N (constant), the branches (homogeneous samples), their interaction (none), and the decision point (after all N finish); the elastic harness relaxes all five. Prior use of the exact phrase is a small unrelated state-machine harness project; Harness.io is a different domain.

A branch is a fork of a session and the relevant executable environment. The initial version preserves session and filesystem state, starts a fresh executor, and makes the environment guarantees explicit. VM memory and live-process preservation belong to a later version. A branch normally inherits the session history; a candidate fresh-context mode can omit the parent's reasoning while preserving the task and executable workspace (§4). A detailed child assignment is optional: the child can discover their own useful continuation. Define the snapshot boundary for the chosen backend before selecting models and tasks.

A join incorporates selected artifacts, findings, and context from another branch. Integration happens through agent communication (§5); preserve provenance and validate the resulting artifact. Arbitrary process memories are not assumed to be automatically mergeable.

Every session has identical privileges and capabilities. They may work, fork, message peers, incorporate results, or stop themselves. A session does not become a privileged coordinator (the root differs only in delivering the result to the user) and cannot unilaterally kill other sessions. When the base session finishes a turn, they return to the user as normal; the interface indicates when forks are still working and keeps them accessible, and subsequent peer messages may arrive at the base session later. Having checked the other sessions before returning, the base session can report their status naturally without special waiting code. The runtime enforces shared resource limits and handles environment lifecycle cleanup.

Part II — Within modest compute: prompts, adapters, small models, and the fork backends

3. Stage 0: prompt-learned organization

Stage 0 is prompt-only fork/join and the primary deliverable. Fix a strong tool-enabled model and the runtime; modify the harness system prompt to expose fork/message/end and a compact loom view; search a population of prompts genetically on development tasks. Fitness is delivered correctness minus a cost penalty, on paired tasks, with repeated seeds and held-out evaluation. Baselines: single session, fixed-N=5, serial retry at matched budget, and the untrained model without the tools. No training. The stage answers whether the interface is usable and whether transparent best-of-N beats the baselines at matched cost. Prompt-only organization cannot exceed the model's ability to judge when to fork and which result to keep; a plateau is a finding, not a failure. Reuse the genetic prompt-search discipline from the project-manager document-generation experiment: a population rather than one running best, sparse cumulative evaluation, stored evaluations never repeated, held-out cases, and the search cost counted in the budget.

- Vary only the system prompt and few-shot examples, with sections for when to fork, how many, what to send, when to join, when to stop, and how to read the loom.
- Prompt-only successes and failures inform the interface and the training design. Winning prompts and traces can supply a teacher policy and distillation targets; failure to beat fixed-N through prompting does not gate training.
- Run a capability gradient: strong model prompt-learned, then a mid-size open model (7B-32B, local) prompt-learned, then distill to the 0.5B-3B student. This separates "the interface is learnable" from "a tiny model can execute it."
- Teacher-checkpoint intervention is a separate deferred mechanism (§9.4): sample K interventions from one checkpoint, execute each in a fork, select by outcome.

3.1 Loom observation for the fork decision

The deciding session needs a compact observation, not the full loom: branch ids, goals, status, last emission as a short string, per-branch cost in tokens, wall clock and disk, remaining budget, depth, and the marginal cost of one more fork. Without cost there is no value-of-computation decision; without the summary the model cannot see what is already being tried.

Any branch's full trace is readable on demand (history://<branch> or an equivalent tool). Inspection is an ordinary tool call in the trace, not an elicitation prompt, and the cost term rewards fewer of them, so the model learns both to infer the loom from summaries and to ask for a trace when the summaries are insufficient. Use emissions, not transcripts, and keep the observation small enough for a 0.5B-3B student.

3.2 The existing session label as a peer-facing summary

The summary already exists: the few-word description a session generates for a picker or status bar, derived from their task or current activity. It is not trained today. Putting it in the loom observation makes it the peer's default view of what a branch is doing, so it becomes load-bearing and therefore trainable as a communication channel.

There is no separate summarization objective and no sufficiency measure. The label is part of the trace: traces that deliver a correct result at acceptable cost are selected for training and their labels are reinforced with the rest of the decisions, while misleading labels tend to appear in traces that fail, recover, or are filtered out.

- Emit the label and cost at every turn or branch boundary, appended at the turn tail so earlier transcript tokens never change and KV-cache prefixes stay valid across forks.
- Keep inspection available but do not score the label against it; inspection is learned from the same outcome and cost signal.
- Write the label for the peer's decision, not only the human's picker: "Testing retry backoff" serves both; "exploring alternative A because the first failed on the boundary case" serves the peer better and still reads in a picker. The runtime keeps the same field and lets the model choose the content.

4. MVP: ephemeral btrfs filesystem forks, no explicit join

The MVP is one new primitive, a filesystem fork that accompanies the session fork, plus the existing message passing. The snapshot mechanism is btrfs: a subvolume snapshot gives the child a stable fork-time view while the source keeps operating and changing, and snapshots of snapshots allow arbitrary branching. Overlays and the earlier fork tools (§14.1) handle arbitrary branching over a live filesystem worse. VM and live-process functionality are deferred (§11). There is no join operation: a branch sends messages and artifact references, and the recipient imports what they want.

- Session: inherit the transcript by default (fresh-context mode below); carry the applicable session state; start a fresh executor.
- Filesystem: a branch workspace that is a CoW view of the selected snapshot (`from_snapshot`, defaulting to the parent's current workspace, or targeting the root or an earlier fork snapshot for explicit backtracking). The branch writes only inside its own directory; everything inherited is read-only.
- Visibility: branch directories live under a runtime-managed cache path, not in the project; loom_status() shows them on request; GC reclaims them.
- Lifecycle: end, message, stop. No merge step. A fork can also serve as optional task-based compaction: a session spins off a noisy investigative subtask into a child and receives only the child's end() summary on their own append-only transcript.

Context modes (candidate interface, also an ablation):
- Inherited: retain the session's accumulated understanding and evidence. The parent asks for another continuation without identifying a specialist or the missing knowledge.
- Fresh: retain the original task, environment guarantees, budget, and artifact access; omit the parent's reasoning history. Useful when uncertainty is high or the agent suspects a wild goose chase. No narrow delegation brief is required.

Both modes fork the current workspace or an earlier snapshot via `from_snapshot`. Fresh context does not undo filesystem changes; when the workspace has been modified since root, the fork result carries a brief dirty-tree indicator so a fresh-context child does not assume a pristine checkout. Which mode and snapshot target to use, what to communicate, and how to combine the evidence are policy choices the swarm can learn, so the ablation is also an action.

Write isolation:
- btrfs only. The live base is never mounted read-only and never used directly as a branch root. Capture a read-only snapshot, then create the branch as a writable snapshot of the capture: `btrfs subvolume snapshot -r base snap-<id>`, then `btrfs subvolume snapshot snap-<id> branch-<id>`. The base continues changing, the branch's fork-time view stays fixed, and snapshot of a snapshot gives recursive block-level CoW. Where a separate immutable capture is not needed, `btrfs subvolume snapshot base branch-<id>` suffices.
- Mount namespace: bind the branch subvolume read-write at the canonical workspace path so `cwd`, virtualenvs, build caches, and absolute paths in the inherited transcript stay valid. Maintain a global read-only mapping of all loom directories (for example `/run/weft/branches/<id>`) for cross-branch inspection, and put the originator's loom directory in cross-session message headers so recipients can translate paths and inspect the sender's work. Everything else outside the branch is read-only or absent.
- Access control: run the branch process as a user or namespace that cannot write the parent or base; mount the host project read-only; no shared writable bind mounts. The branch's writable set is exactly its subvolume.
- External side effects still escape: network, shared services, host-mounted sockets. Isolate network per branch or treat external state as shared.

Rejected for the MVP: overlayfs and fuse-overlayfs, whose lowerdir must be an immutable capture (a read-only view of a still-changing source is undefined behavior); a snapshot-then-overlay stack, which adds a copy-up layer and no capability; XFS reflink, which is block-level but gives no atomic tree snapshot of a live workspace. Measure btrfs snapshot cost and its effect on active source operations.

Block-level CoW on the existing disk (verified on this host): a sparse btrfs loopback image on the existing ext4, mounted through a privileged storage container with a shared bind mount. Rootless user namespaces are blocked by AppArmor (apparmor_restrict_unprivileged_userns=1), so unshare and rootless podman cannot mount btrfs; rootful Docker can, and the user is in the docker group. Verified inside a privileged container: mkfs.btrfs on a 256 MB sparse file, mount -o loop, subvolume create and snapshot. Verified propagation to the host with --mount type=bind,source=<host dir>,target=<mount>,bind-propagation=rshared; the host then sees the directory as btrfs and can create snapshots. The runtime stays on the host; the container is only the mount provider. Caveats: the sidecar must stay alive or the mount disappears; the loop file cannot shrink easily, so size it generously; docker-group access is root-equivalent, so this is a trust boundary; keep the sidecar minimal and offline.

GC is tree-aware (a parent's layer cannot be deleted while a child references it; delete ended leaves, archive artifacts first, compact deep chains when lookup cost grows), accounted with btrfs qgroups or compsize, and crash-safe (mark branches orphaned on startup and reclaim them).

Not solved: live processes, in-memory state, warm page cache, exact mid-flight state. Add a service manifest to restart declared services; stateful services need per-branch instances or dump/restore.

Tests: write in the child, fork a grandchild, read the write, and confirm the parent does not see it; change the parent after a fork and confirm the child keeps their fork-time contents while source operations continue across capture; attempt a write outside the branch directory and fail; confirm shared blocks are not duplicated, fork latency scales with file count rather than data size, cleanup leaves no residue, and nested depth and large files behave.

4.1 Fork transparency and explicit branch identity

Fork transparency means inherited executable state is ready to use within the backend's declared guarantees. The child receives their branch identity, context mode, remaining budget, and environment guarantees automatically, and may have an open-ended purpose. To preserve KV-cache reuse, the system prompt and all pre-fork transcript tokens stay byte-identical between parent and child: identity, mode, budget, and any dirty-tree notice enter only in the appended `fork` tool result at the tail of the shared prefix. The runtime defines where the copied transcript resumes and which fork result the child sees; provenance stays in the runtime log, with loom_status() for detail on demand.

The payoff is a small action space (fork, message, end, with no setup ritual to learn or fail), clean credit assignment (a branch failure is the branch's decisions, not a setup mistake), a fair fixed-N comparison from the same environment, and training data free of environment-management noise; the complexity lives in deterministic runtime code.

Every backend declares the guarantees it provides. The btrfs backend matches the transcript and files but not running processes or ports; the seams a VM backend must own are in §11. Train on a backend whose guarantees match the tasks, so the policy does not learn backend-specific rituals. Infrastructure failures (snapshot, restore, reconnect) are recorded separately and excluded from policy training.

4.2 Write-isolation recipe

Isolation is kernel-enforced; no custom filesystem is needed. The fork tools that implement parts of this (BranchFS/BranchContext, LayerFS, simgit, Waypoint, pVisor, Sandlock) are in §14.1; borrow their isolation design, not their lifecycles.

- Run the branch process in its own mount namespace: branch subvolume read-write at the workspace path, parent or base read-only only if it must be visible, everything else read-only or absent. BranchFS gives each agent a mount namespace with only their @branch path as workspace root.
- Add a user namespace and a distinct UID so the branch cannot write parent-owned paths even if a mount is misconfigured.
- Add Landlock and seccomp (for example sandlock) as defense in depth.
- Per-branch /tmp, /dev/shm, and cache directories; never share a writable bind mount. Host mounts are external state and are not forked.
- Test: writing outside the branch directory fails with EROFS or EACCES, including through symlinks, `..`, and absolute paths.
- Local tools: bwrap, unshare, newuidmap; no root for the user-namespace path. fuse-overlayfs is not used.

The message-passing model needs a writable branch directory with a stable fork-time base and read access to the branch from the parent, not commit-to-parent. btrfs gives this directly; simgit is git-only, BranchFS and LayerFS are commit-oriented, and Waypoint needs root and CRIU. Expose only fork/discard/read, let messages carry artifact references, and do not implement merge.

5. Join protocol: message-based integration

Join is not a merge. A branch that stops emits a summary to the parent and subscribed peers; recipients decide what to import.

- end(summary, artifacts, status): the branch stops; the runtime records an emission with provenance, cost, and artifact references, and keeps the branch snapshot readable.
- send(branch, message) / broadcast(message) (opt-in subscriptions only): delivered to an inbox and seen at the recipient's next turn. Headers carry the originator's global loom directory so the recipient can translate paths and inspect the sender's files. An interrupt flag delivers at the next tool boundary, never mid-generation. consult(branch, question) forks the recipient, full transcript and btrfs workspace, so they can both run the interruption and not run it: the quarantined copy verifies the sender's claims in the recipient's own state of mind and workspace, answers the caller, and decides whether to forward a verified message to the live recipient, who stays uninterrupted and unpolluted if the claim does not hold up.
- import_artifact(branch, path|patch): copies files or applies a patch into the recipient's filesystem under an explicit conflict policy; validate by running tests; record the source emission and content hash.
- loom_status(): a compact table of branches (id, goal, status, last emission, cost, artifact references), never full transcripts; the parent's working model of the loom and the main defense against context blow-up in small models.
- Runtime rules: a branch cannot kill a peer but may request_stop. A parent cannot end with live children unless the parent explicitly orphans them (reparent to grandparent or root). Crashed or timed-out branches get synthetic emissions (exit status, artifacts, partial summary). Quiescence and timeout prevent deadlock. Budget is visible to the policy and enforced by the runtime.

The summary is the message, artifacts are pulled by reference, validation is the acceptance test, and every join decision is a typed tool call that can be prompted and trained.

6. Harness support for message passing

- OMP: first-class peer messaging through hub (send, wait, inbox, list, broadcast, replyTo, await). Subagents are addressable peers; parked agents revive on a direct send; messages arrive as non-interrupting asides while running and start a turn when idle. Task subagents have filesystem isolation (task.isolation.backend: overlayfs, fuse-overlayfs, btrfs, reflink, zfs, apfs, block-clone, rcopy), batch fan-out (task.batch), parking (task.agentIdleTtlMs), and artifacts (agent://, history://). Gap: /fork copies the session transcript and artifacts but not the filesystem; task(isolated) isolates the filesystem but starts a fresh context and is torn down at completion. The combined session-plus-environment fork is the missing primitive.
- Claude Code: agent teams (experimental) with per-agent mailboxes, SendMessage, ListAgents, shared task lists, and direct teammate messaging, including across machines. Unverified: the Agent tool's fork mode inherits the full conversation and the same call accepts `isolation: worktree`, which may make session fork and environment isolation one call; verify before relying on the gap. Even so, the worktree is a clean git checkout of `HEAD`, not a CoW filesystem fork: it drops uncommitted edits, untracked files, and gitignored build or environment state (`.venv`, `node_modules`, compiled artifacts) accumulated before a mid-task fork.
- OpenCode: subagents and child sessions; no first-class peer messaging. Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor, Aider, and the other harnesses in plan-delivery: parent-child delegation or background tasks; no first-class peer messaging verified.

Build on OMP first: it already supplies messaging, isolation backends including btrfs, fan-out, parking, and artifacts. Add a fork tool that creates the session continuation, snapshots the workspace as a btrfs subvolume, and registers the child in the hub; the rest is prompt and policy. Claude Code is the alternative if agent teams are preferred, but its environment fork is a worktree, not CoW.

6.1 OMP copy lifecycle and access

OMP has two separate copies. /fork copies the session JSONL, a new header (new id, parentSession), and the artifacts directory into a new session namespace; cwd and workspace are unchanged, and the fork persists until /drop with no automatic GC. task(isolated) creates a private workspace through pi-iso (kernel overlay with fuse-overlayfs/fusermount fallback on Linux, APFS/Btrfs/ZFS/reflink clones, ProjFS, or recursive copy); the subagent gets a fresh context, runs there, commits to a branch (omp/task/<id>) or captures <id>.patch, and the workspace is always cleaned up. Isolated agents are torn down at completion and are not revivable; the transcript stays readable via history://. Non-isolated subagents share the parent cwd and stay alive; idle agents park after task.agentIdleTtlMs (7 min default), keeping the AgentRef and session JSONL, and revive on a hub message or an Agent Hub action.

The isolated workspace is private to the branch; other agents can read its output (agent://<id>), transcript (history://<id>), patch (<id>.patch), or merged branch, not the live workspace. local:// is shared across the parent and subagents. The hub roster is process-global: peers are agents in the same omp process/session tree; hub send revives a parked agent; broadcast targets visible live peers; mailboxes cap at 100 messages. Collab guests can view transcripts and control host subagents but are not peers.

The gap: OMP's isolated branches are not revivable, and its revivable branches are not isolated. The combined fork needs a new lifecycle: retain the branch workspace, register the branch as a revivable peer, and GC on end or space pressure according to the backend's dependencies. That is the tool to add.

7. Baselines, targets, and interpretation

For fixed N, begin with five independent attempts. Record both whether any candidate passes the evaluator (pass-at-five) and the correctness of the one answer an implementable model-and-tools selection procedure delivers. Selection procedures get only the evidence available during execution; held-out evaluator results remain evaluation evidence.

Pass-at-five is an engineering reference and target, not the definition of success. The adaptive system should match or exceed that benefit while handling its own selection and integration at lower cost than fixed-N, and may beat independent attempts by combining discoveries across branches.

Diagnose failures separately: no branch produced a correct solution; a correct solution existed but was not selected; incorporation damaged a useful result. Each points at generation, coordination, selection, or merging. Negative measurements remain meaningful when they motivate iterations rather than abandoning the direction.

Measure delivered correctness, total cost, and elapsed time over several budgets. A useful result extends the Pareto frontier on accuracy, cost, or latency; slightly lower accuracy can still be competitive on cost or latency. Score the delivered artifact and account for every branch's work. Report training and data-generation costs separately from deployment costs, both inside the initial trial's budget.

Dollar cost is a primary axis, and the most interesting outcome is equal delivered correctness and task latency while serving inference at a lower dollars-per-token point. Report total dollars per delivered result alongside input/output token counts, cache usage, effective token prices, environment cost, and selection/communication/integration work; lower token prices and lower task cost are separate measurements, and additional tokens belong in the latter. For local serving, state the hardware-cost basis and measure utilization and queueing. Model and task selection start once the tool exists, on ordinary tasks with a best-of-N benefit rather than a curated set of decomposition opportunities.

8. Plan of work and deliverables

The work runs as parallel tracks; the btrfs fork tool is the shared dependency.

- Fork backends (§4, §6, §11). Build the btrfs fork tool on OMP first: session continuation, subvolume snapshot, hub registration, GC. The VM backend is developed in parallel against the §11 conformance recipe and gates nothing below; once it passes, Stage 0 can also run on it for tasks that need live processes.
- Stage 0 (§3, §7). Starts when the btrfs fork tool runs. The initial target is transparent best-of-N: the user asks once, the harness exposes fork/message/end, the runtime isolates execution, the swarm selects and integrates, and the user sees one result. No fine-tuning. Prompt-only comes first because it tests the interface before any training compute is spent, it is the product surface users run, it costs inference and runtime only, and it produces the traces and winning prompts a later training stage would distill.
- Model specialization (§12.1). The prompt-differentiated variant runs alongside Stage 0 from the start and needs only a target on `fork`. Weight-differentiated targets follow: adapters first, then small-model checkpoints from the training track.
- Training track (§9). Stage A plain distillation and its infrastructure can start independently; Stages B and C wait on loom traces from Stage 0. Successful prompt-only organization is not a prerequisite.
- Jev-assisted organization (§10). No training; a comparison condition once untrained and trained students exist.
- Reading (§14). Step 1 alongside the fork tool, step 2 during systems work, steps 3 and 4 once the tool exists, steps 5 and 6 when their tracks open.

Primary deliverables: the modified harness system prompt, the fork/message/end tool schema, the runtime, the fork backend, and the Stage 0 results (delivered correctness, cost, and latency against single-session, fixed-N, and serial-retry baselines, with the prompt-search record and cost accounting). Also deliver the reproducible runtime and configurations, recorded looms, held-out evaluation results, failure diagnoses, and complete cost accounting; if the training track is pursued, selected training examples, student checkpoints, validated perturbation generators, and paired task manifests. Preserve enough records for a frontier-model session to inspect the loom conversationally and help a human identify targeted interventions; a bespoke observability interface is not a prerequisite.

9. Training track

The training track (Stage A onward) is informed by Stage 0 but does not wait on it. One training task family first: coding, with multiple task instances and separate training, development, and held-out evaluation splits; multi-task training is a possible later step. The initial experiment uses full student fine-tuning; adapters are the first weight-differentiated specialization targets (§12.1), and the Jev-assisted variant (§10) is a separate no-training comparison. Establish tool-enabled coding baselines first: Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B-Instruct is a candidate for a cheap initial probe, with 1.5–3B alternatives; select executable coding and repair tasks with reliable outcome checks; start with bounded tasks and expand to repository work, with ProgramBench when the system is ready for that scale.

Stage A: plain DeepSeek distillation and container infrastructure, in parallel. Generate ordinary DeepSeek coding outputs or serial tool-use traces on training-split tasks, without loom interventions, and fully fine-tune the student on them. This exercises data collection, formatting, training, checkpoint export, inference, and evaluation, and is preserved as the stronger-teacher distillation control. Independently build and validate the forkable container/session infrastructure and join semantics; the plain-distillation run need not wait for the loom runtime.

Stage B: DeepSeek-guided fork/join, after both Stage A tracks work. At execution checkpoints DeepSeek chooses whether and how to intervene, supplying a continuation, including legal fork/join actions, from only the information available at that checkpoint. Execute the actions and resume the weaker model; record actual tool results and branch outcomes rather than inventing successful continuations. Teacher intervention uses the same session capabilities and gains no authority to terminate peers. Train a separate student from the same initial checkpoint and compare them with the matched plain-distillation control. Strategy prompts govern insertion points and insertions: begin with a small set and let the Stage 0 genetic search mutate and select them by the completed looms they produce, with delivered correctness and total resource consumption as fitness, counting unsuccessful branches, search, filtering, and teacher calls, and repeating evaluations to separate improvements from sampling noise.

Stage C: same-model self-bootstrapping. Replace DeepSeek's data-generation and intervention role with the frozen checkpoint being improved, and compare self-generated serial traces with self-generated loom traces. Keep stronger-model proposals, intervention selection, and learned judging out of this condition; executable training-task feedback remains available. Log the source of every decision so stronger-teacher assistance cannot silently enter the result. This is the central control for whether gains arise without importing a stronger teacher's capabilities.

For every condition, fine-tune all student weights on selected useful decision and continuation examples, including useful failed experiments followed by revision or self-stopping; do not imitate every action from a successful loom; retain ordinary coding behavior and check for regressions; derive each example from information actually available to the session that produced it. Sibling branches forked from the same snapshot share an exact token and filesystem prefix up to the fork turn, so the recorded loom supplies a counterfactual comparison group at each branch point: intra-tree group-relative advantage across siblings (Tree-GRPO, TreePO) assigns step-level credit to post-fork continuations and to whether the fork paid for its cost, without a separate critic or retrospective teacher rollbacks. Where a branch is used for task-based context compaction, traces that succeed on the returned emission while keeping the parent's active context small can be weighted accordingly (FoldGRPO).

Evaluate trained students on held-out tasks without DeepSeek or other extra decision models. One successful self-bootstrap round demonstrates self-training or self-distillation; a claim of recursive self-improvement requires later checkpoints to generate useful training data for further generations with sustained measured gains under comparable budgets.

Model size is an experimental variable. A student barely able to write code may be unable to recognize when to fork, evaluate other branches, or integrate results; a negative result on a tiny student leads to diagnosis and larger students within separately established budgets, not to rejecting the architecture.

9.1 Training baselines and controls

Measure an untrained single session, the untrained student with the loom primitives, a fixed-N implementation, and the trained adaptive loom, on the same task starts with comparable tools and budgets. Teacher-assisted execution is a separate diagnostic condition.

The training controls form a two-by-two: DeepSeek-generated ordinary traces; DeepSeek-guided loom traces; same-model ordinary traces; same-model loom traces. Start each run from the identical student checkpoint; match processed training tokens, supervised target-token counts as closely as practical, optimizer settings, update budgets, task distributions, and evaluation resources, and disclose remaining differences. Ordinary DeepSeek outputs on benchmark training tasks are a real baseline, so a gain over the untouched student alone is not attributed to loom training. Account separately for trace-generation, candidate-search, filtering, and selection costs, and use a matched generation-budget comparison to check whether gains come from more compute or more selected data. The same-model serial versus same-model loom comparison isolates organization from stronger-teacher transfer. Stage the four conditions within the budget rather than launching all of them before the pipeline works.

9.2 Generalization and curriculum

Train on the single coding family and measure held-out coding performance; then evaluate the frozen checkpoint on unseen task families without further training, against the same untrained and plain-fine-tuned controls. Candidate transfer settings: tool-based data analysis, environment troubleshooting, and evidence-gathering tasks with verifiable outcomes, keeping the fork/join interface fixed while task content and, later, tool sets and horizons vary. Report absolute performance and improvement over the controls; a model with no competence in a transfer task gives an inconclusive result. Transfer across several settings would support reusable organization behavior without establishing universal planning competence.

Task perturbations, where a reliable validity check exists, begin with inexpensive seeded transformations of coding tasks. Behavior-preserving variants (consistent identifier renaming, formatting, reordering independent definitions, equivalent task descriptions) are checked against the task contract, since reflection, public APIs, imports, or order-dependent execution can invalidate them. Changed-solution variants (input ranges, boundary conditions, data schemas, requirements) update reference solutions and tests together or derive expected behavior from a trusted executable specification or a checkable relation to the original; for these, unchanged output is not the desired invariant. Controlled execution perturbations, such as new evidence arriving at a checkpoint, come later and stay distinct from difficulty increases.

Rules: split source tasks before creating variants and keep every original with all its variants in one split; reserve some transformation families or compositions for evaluation; version the generators, record seeds, and validate variants before comparing results; prefer deterministic transformations, and record provenance and validate independently when a model generates a variant; evaluation variants and their answers never enter trace generation, filtering, prompt search, or training; training-time augmentation is a later, separately controlled experiment. Run original/perturbed pairs across the untrained, plain-distillation, guided-loom, self-training, and self-loom conditions at matched budgets, measuring delivered correctness, the change under perturbation, cost, latency, and the fork/join decisions involved, and treat variants of one source task as related observations. Robustness differences can reveal reliance on benchmark wording or teacher-specific patterns but cannot prove or rule out distillation on their own; read them with the matched teacher-source controls and the same-model experiment.

If single-family training does not transfer, test a curriculum of a few task families, keeping some families entirely out of training and the transfer-development tasks separate from final evaluations, and account for the added budget. Multi-task training is not a prerequisite for the first trial.

9.3 Resources and estimates

Stage 0 costs are inference and runtime only: model calls, the fork backend, storage, and evaluation. The $1,000 ceiling applies to the full trial if the training track is pursued, including DeepSeek calls, rentals, storage, environments, training, and evaluation; the prompt-only stage should cost far less. Use the existing Spark for full fine-tuning and the RTX 3080 Ti and RTX 3070 as possible independent student-inference workers; confirm the Spark's configuration and the CPU RAM and disk available for forked environments. In the DeepSeek phases plan for up to ten concurrent API sessions with explicit token and spending caps and a bounded initial run; the self-bootstrap condition uses the student checkpoint for those decisions. Reserve resources for matched controls and evaluation rather than one trained condition.

A costing placeholder is 25,000 examples averaging 4,000 processed tokens, 100 million training tokens per pass; AsyncThink's roughly 25,000–43,000 bootstrap pairs supplied the scale, and its later RL stage and different tasks mean it is not evidence that this task needs or will succeed with that amount. Whether 4,000 tokens is adequate for these coding tasks is doubtful: measure representative real session contexts, count repeated context processing, examples from all branches, training passes, and full-loom generation and evaluation, and recompute memory and time estimates from those measurements. Day/week/month model-size estimates are speculative until throughput is measured. Renting a larger interconnected GPU node is an option if a larger-student experiment is justified; recheck prices, availability, training memory, and total budget first. The first local run exists to establish actual throughput, cost, and a learning signal.

9.4 Elicitation and the teacher as labelers

Elicitation is the teacher-free labeler. At each decision point the runtime pauses and asks a generic introspection question: reasons a session might branch (uncertainty about the approach, several plausible strategies, independent subproblems, a result that is expensive to verify, remaining budget) and reasons not to (the marginal branch is unlikely to pay for itself, the evidence is already sufficient, the budget is nearly spent); do you want to branch, and why? The model answers in the trace and, on yes, forks from the current state.

- The prompt is generic and presents the space of reasons, not the answer. Reasons against are required; without them the scaffold teaches an always-fork bias.
- The elicited reasoning, the decision, and the outcome form the training trace. Train the model to produce the introspection and the decision without the scaffold; the scaffold is a context-distillation device.
- The decision is the label; delivered correctness and cost filter and weight it. Without an outcome signal, elicitation only teaches the model to answer the question, not to answer it well.
- Keep no-branch decisions in the data, balanced against forks, and measure the fork rate against a neutral prompt to detect scaffold-induced over-branching.

The teacher is retrospective: they inspect the trace afterward and insert at a point they judge reasonable, which requires every session's state at that step, so storage is O(sessions x steps x state) and grows with the tree; sparse checkpoints reduce storage but limit insertion points. Elicitation is prospective: it asks at the current frontier, a yes forks from the present state, and its cost is latency proportional to elicitation frequency, not storage. The teacher has hindsight and can label points the student skipped; elicitation can only ask now.

Use the teacher to seed a small set of good decisions and elicitation to scale the data. Do not run the teacher over the full loom, and do not build the programmatic teacher flow or full historical checkpointing until elicitation results show where they are needed; then retain checkpoints only for the decisions elicitation gets wrong. A weak model may branch too often or too rarely at first; if elicitation alone yields no usable signal, the teacher seeds and elicitation scales from there.

10. Jev-assisted organization without training

Keep a Jev-assisted variant as a distinct experiment. Each session can consult the same classifier about concrete available fork/join actions: the session or surrounding software supplies candidate actions and parameters, Jev selects or scores them, and the runtime records an explicit continuation in the trace, performs the action, and resumes. This tests whether a general-purpose decision model provides useful organization without student training; Jev is not a privileged manager. Compare the variant with the untrained and trained students, counting classifier input processing and all branch work. If it works, a learned or distilled decision policy becomes a possible cost optimization.

11. Fork mechanisms: deferred VM design

The memory-layer design below is a candidate for a later VM backend, not an MVP requirement. Its target is to share read-only physical pages between a child and their immediate parent, where the parent may itself be a COW layer over its own parent: a tree of layers, not a star of children over one host snapshot. A fork of a fork must see the intermediate branch's changes and share that branch's pages read-only, copying only pages written after that fork. Each fork creates a memory layer whose reads fall through to the parent layer and whose writes allocate in the child layer, so unchanged pages are shared transitively. Guest page tables are ordinary guest memory and layer like any other page; the host EPT/NPT is per-VM, the one mapping not shared, small relative to RAM, and lazily populated. The disk uses the same layered structure (reflink or overlayfs lower layers, per-branch upper layers). Layers compose recursively and reads walk the chain, so bound the depth and compact offline.

Containers do not provide this: CRIU, gVisor checkpoint/restore, and the Kubernetes container checkpoint API copy memory and restore into a new container, with no COW sharing and no cheap fork; Docker/Podman checkpoint is a migration primitive; process-level fork COWs data pages but copies page tables, and page-table COW needs kernel changes. Use microVM fork, where the guest page tables are just COW memory. The verified implementations, classified by whether they layer on the immediate parent or re-base on the child, are catalogued in §14.6.

The seams the runtime owns on this backend, so that fork transparency (§4.1) holds for running processes as well as files:
- IO policy: quiesce the session at a turn boundary, flush and drain disk writes, drop external network connections, snapshot, restore, then run the reconnect hook. Never discard dirty disk writes without flushing; drop external network, not disk or in-guest loopback.
- Connections: loopback connections with both endpoints inside the guest survive in the snapshot; the criterion is the VM boundary, not the address family. Host port-forwards, host-mounted Unix sockets, vsock, and host-run services cross the boundary and are re-established per branch. Each branch gets a network namespace with the same addresses and ports and an egress policy; a reconnect proxy re-establishes external connections underneath.
- Stable hostname, cwd, environment, and process tree.
- Reseeded RNG and a defined clock policy; branches must not share entropy or assume wall-clock continuity.
- External shared state is isolated per branch (per-branch service instances, database snapshots) or surfaced honestly, and external effects (remote APIs, git remotes) are recorded. Transparency applies inside the machine, not to the outside world.

Costs and hazards: snapshot capture, not restore, dominates a fork from a running branch (full capture faults and writes all RAM; diff capture writes only dirty pages), so fork from a clean checkpoint at a tool boundary. Memory accounting must count shared pages once and charge unique pages per branch, or fan-out cost is overstated and budgets are wrong. Snapshot files contain all memory, including secrets; restrict access and encrypt at rest. Cross-host forks ship memory and disk, so keep forks on the parent's host and send only emissions across machines; CPU feature and GIC compatibility constrain restore across hosts. GPU state does not fork cleanly; keep model inference outside the forked session and fork only tool and environment state.

Verification recipe for the layered property (run before committing to a backend):
1. In the child, write a known pattern to a page that was unchanged at the child's fork, then fork a grandchild. The grandchild must read the child's pattern, not the root's. This catches re-basing that silently drops intermediate state.
2. Measure host shared and private memory (PSS-like accounting, or COW fault counts) before and after forking N grandchildren. Pages the child never wrote must not be duplicated; the grandchild's private memory must scale with their own writes, not with the child's private set.
3. Measure fork latency against dirty MB since the parent layer. A layered backend's grandchild fork cost scales with the child's dirty pages; a re-basing backend scales with the child's total private memory.
4. Fork a chain at least three deep and read pages written at each level; then compact and re-read. This catches chain-depth and compaction bugs.

Investigate Cloud Hypervisor, Firecracker, microsandbox, E2B, smolvm, and forkd against the recipe (§14.6). Verify cross-generation physical-memory sharing separately from filesystem reflinks or shared restores of one snapshot; mechanism descriptions alone do not establish the stronger property. Instrument fork latency versus dirty MB, fan-out, shared versus private pages, and emission bytes. This research does not gate the filesystem MVP.

11.1 Scaling to thousands of branches (deferred VM backend)

The btrfs MVP scales by subvolume count, snapshot depth, and qgroup accounting; its limits are inodes, metadata, and shared-extent reference counts, not vCPUs or guest memory. On the VM backend, fork transparency is what makes a large loom affordable: a branch's marginal cost is its unique dirty pages plus vCPU time, not an environment bootstrap. At 1,000 branches the limits are unique memory, CPU overcommit, host thread/FD/TAP limits, snapshot storage, and the pause cost of snapshotting an active parent; thousands fit on one large host only if most branches are small and short-lived, otherwise the loom spans hosts. The per-branch cost model and the requirements at that scale (same-host placement, recursive layered trees, parking and eviction, admission control, hierarchical loom_status, failure handling, security, a multi-host scheduler) are in §14.6. The bottleneck then shifts from fork cost to inference and coordination: a thousand branches pay off only if most are short-lived and the policy prunes early, so the value-of-computation decision becomes the main learned skill.

Per-node fork plus emissions-only networking is the C7/C10 shape. Omerta's VM-per-task model becomes fork-per-branch if the provider exposes snapshot/restore; the Linux KVM path (Firecracker/Cloud Hypervisor) has the COW primitive, while Apple Virtualization.framework save/restore does not share memory across restores.

Part III — Frontier-scale compute and the broader hypothesis

12. Broader hypothesis

The larger bet is that learning to organize branches becomes learning how to investigate a problem. The system can generate alternatives, run experiments to discriminate among them, revise assumptions, exchange evidence, and reconsider which possibilities deserve more computation. A branch can implement a solution or investigate whether the problem itself has been framed correctly.

The “space of all plans” is the ideal search domain, explored under resource constraints. Useful alternatives and distinctions can be discovered during execution. Plans remain revisable resources. Work ends when the actual result satisfies the relevant acceptance criteria, which may be clarified through feedback during the task; completing an earlier written plan is not sufficient by itself.

The computer-architecture analogy is dynamic task parallelism: a session discovers additional work and creates peers with the same capabilities. Management can recurse without a special manager class. The economic conjecture is that useful additional parallel computation can offer better returns than strengthening one execution stream. No universal exponential cost law is assumed.

A further hypothesis is that learned organization can let capable systems exploit many modest computers with cheaper interconnects. That requires substantial local work between exchanges and sensible decisions about placement, state transfer, and communication. These are future hypotheses, not established results of the initial experiment. Suchman's work inspired the discussion; the branch-management architecture is our engineering extension.

12.1 Transparent and trainable model specialization

The next step toward frontier performance on distributed hardware is to let forks target different models while one base model controls the root session. Candidate targets may be differentiated by weights (distinct checkpoints or adapters, potentially resident on different machines) or by prompting.

The intuition is MoE performance and active-parameter cost on loosely coupled hardware. Token-level MoE routes at every layer and token, which requires high-bandwidth all-to-all interconnects. Moving the routing decision to `fork` shifts communication to branch boundaries and compact emissions, where each branch does substantial local work over their inherited session and workspace before reporting back.

As in the MVP, this remains transparent and trainable rather than relying on prescribed specialist roles or a hand-built router. Because the child still inherits the session state and executable workspace, targeting a different model does not fall back to lossy prose delegation, and the user still sees one root session. Which model or prompt specialization to target on a fork is part of the learned organization policy, priced on the same outcome, dollar, and latency frontier. Suggestions, elicitation, or a stronger teacher can bootstrap the initial routing choices; prompt-differentiated targets can exercise the interface before weight-differentiated models are introduced, and successful branch trajectories can later specialize the target models themselves.

The compute ladder matters. Prompt-differentiated targets cost inference only. Adapters are within reach on local hardware and are the first weight-differentiated targets; full fine-tuning of small students (§9) follows. The hope on that ladder is that a specialized swarm of 8B to 32B models reaches near-frontier, or slightly lagging, performance on modest hardware. Performance well beyond the frontier would need the organization policy trained into the frontier model itself. That is frontier-scale compute and outside what this plan can run, and it is why the primitive is designed to be trainable rather than prescribed.

12.2 Follow-ups

Follow-ups attach to the tracks in §8 rather than to one order. On the training track: after Stages A, B, and C and the transfer tests, multi-task training, repeated self-improvement generations, and larger students when small-model results are inconclusive, with adapters and other training-cost optimizations for larger models. On the specialization track: adapters, then weight-differentiated targets specialized from successful branch trajectories, then the frontier-scale question above. On the fork-backend track: the VM backend against the §11 conformance recipe, then learned decision components, distributed placement, and performance across machines with limited interconnects. On Stage 0 itself: harder repository tasks, stronger single-session baselines at matched cost, and longer contexts. The Jev-assisted architecture (§10) runs as its own comparison whenever student checkpoints exist.

14.1 Step 1 — snapshot semantics and harness integration (immediate implementation dependency)

Start with [OMP](https://github.com/can1357/oh-my-pi)'s hub, task-isolation, session-fork, and lifecycle documentation, alongside the gap recorded in §6.1. Then read BranchFS / BranchContext below, the [Btrfs subvolume documentation](https://btrfs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Subvolumes.html), and the [OverlayFS documentation](https://docs.kernel.org/filesystems/overlayfs.html). The specific question is how to retain a stable fork-time view while the source keeps operating and writing. A read-only mount of a changing source is not sufficient: OverlayFS documents changes to underlying filesystems while mounted as undefined behavior. btrfs is the chosen mechanism (§4); measure it for source-write behavior, interruption, cost, and host compatibility. The output of this step is btrfs's measured costs and guarantees; the VM and process-checkpoint literature is step 6.

- BranchFS / BranchContext (multikernel; MIT / Apache-2.0; ASPLOS 2026 Agentic OS Workshop). The closest filesystem primitive: FUSE copy-on-write filesystem, O(1) branch creation (under 350 microseconds, independent of base size), nested branches, per-branch mount namespace isolation, process groups, no root. It has commit-to-parent, first-commit-wins, sibling invalidation, and a frozen origin, which do not match the message-passing, no-join model; borrow the filesystem and isolation design, not the lifecycle. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08199).
- [LayerFS](https://github.com/Ephemeral-AI-Lab/layerfs) (Ephemeral-AI-Lab; MIT; 2.7k stars). Ephemeral workspaces over shared committed history, recursive branches, content-addressed deduplication, instant bootstrap with dependencies, FUSE plus SQLite. Commit/merge oriented, but a branch is a writable workspace and can be read by others; message passing can replace merge. Heavier than needed for a first MVP.
- [simgit](https://github.com/abendrothj/simgit) (MIT). Copy-on-write git worktrees via reflink or fuse-overlayfs; an extra worktree costs metadata, not a copy of the tree; ephemeral GC; no daemon. The closest match to a worktree-based fork, but git-only and no process state.
- Waypoint (Apache-2.0). Branchable agent containers using CRIU plus OverlayFS: checkpoint a running sandbox (files, memory, live terminal sessions) and fork it into many live copies. The closest match to the full session fork without microVMs; requires root, Linux, and CRIU 4.0+ on aarch64.
- StateFork — Branchable Infrastructure for Agent Exploration (Xu et al., Columbia and Google, 2026). Formalizes observation equivalence across four computer-state layers (filesystem, shell/PTY session state, live process/memory via CRIU, and local services) and pairs a logical branching control plane with the Waypoint execution substrate. On Terminal-Bench, branch-based exploration over Waypoint matches pass@20 in under 10 minutes and beats alternative execution substrates by 26% task accuracy and up to 70% faster exploration. Direct evidence that branching physical machine state improves terminal-agent accuracy and latency. StateFork drives snapshots from an external tree-search controller outside the model; Weft exposes the computer-state fork inside the swarm's own action space. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38648).
- ParallelEnv — Parallel Environments for Agents (Tan, Zhang, and Zaharia, ACM CAIS 2026 demo). The closest systems reference found. At each fork, the system captures the agent's Docker container and starts the child from the capture; a pluggable scheduler decides which branches run. Verify the capture mechanism: a plain `docker commit` pauses the container by default, so the claim that the parent continues depends on how ParallelEnv captures state. Read for fork costs at SWE-bench scale. Its fork trigger is fixed (fork when the patch changes), and it runs the SWE-bench harness at fork points and ends the other branches when tests pass. Its reported 48% at $0.50 on 100 SWE-bench Pro instances therefore depends on a golden checker. Weft leaves the fork decision, the evidence, and stopping to the swarm. (Abstract only.) [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3786335.3813189), [CAIS listing](https://www.caisconf.org/program/2026/demos/parallel-environments-for-agents/).
- SpecBuild — Speculative Forking: Turning Idle Wait into Parallel Exploration for Repository Environment Setup (Shi et al., 2026). Parallel environment branches with a first-commit-wins policy: strategy-level forking races alternative setup routes, and command-level forking predicts a slow command's failure and prepares a repair branch in parallel. Reports +6.4 to +12.0 percentage points success and 22.4% to 41.6% lower setup time across three backbones. The key contrast for Weft: it forks from immutable base images, so the lower layer never changes; Weft forks from a live workspace and therefore needs an atomic snapshot (btrfs). Read for the branch budget, the matched-cost ablation against naive best-of-N, and the first-commit-wins join policy. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33129).
- pVisor (Apache-2.0). Staged workspaces with review/apply/drop and a VM-per-container mode; not a fork, but relevant for isolation and review.
- Sandlock (Apache-2.0). Landlock plus seccomp process sandbox with a COW working directory, no root, about 5 ms startup. The confinement layer for a fork (§4.2), not a branching tool.
- getkern/kern. Rootless container runtime for agent tool calls; an isolation reference for the selected executor.
- [mini-SWE-agent](https://github.com/SWE-agent/mini-swe-agent) (SWE-bench team). A minimal companion to SWE-agent: roughly 100 lines of Python implementing a bash-only, linear-history observe-think-act loop with file viewing, editing, and searching. Later versions report above 74% on SWE-bench Verified with no tool hierarchy beyond bash. Not a fork mechanism and not a learned organization policy. Its value here is as a harness-mass control: if a loom built on a heavy scaffold outperforms a single session on the same scaffold, mini-SWE-agent is the cheap check that the gain comes from branching the session and workspace rather than from scaffold bulk. It is also the harness used by the high-throughput parallel attempt in §14.3 (Doubleword), which makes its fixed-N numbers directly comparable to a loom run on the same base loop.

14.2 Step 2 — learned organization (read early to guide the interface)

Read AsyncThink, then the two metareasoning papers, then EnCompass. Together they address learned concurrency, the value of additional computation, and separating agent workflow from search over execution paths. Use them to ask which decisions the primitive should leave available to the agents. Follow with the project's minimal-sufficient-inference review and plan-mind §7b (§13) for context inheritance, emissions, and recursive invocation. This conceptual reading can proceed during systems research without delaying the first usable tool.

- AsyncThink — The Era of Agentic Organization: Learning to Organize with Language Models (Chi et al., 2025). The central conceptual reference, and the data-volume reference in §9.3. Its organizer dynamically assigns sub-queries to workers, incorporates their results, and learns asynchronous thinking structures through training. Read for the action protocol, learning signal, and treatment of concurrency and latency. Weft's questions include inherited executable state and recursive peers with the same capabilities; examine those differences directly rather than assuming results transfer from reasoning tasks. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26658).
- Rational metareasoning — Selecting Computations: Theory and Applications (Hay, Russell, Shimony, and Tolpin, 2012), followed by Learning to Select Computations (Callaway et al., 2018). The first formalizes choosing computations by the expected value of the eventual decision minus the cost of gathering information. The second learns policies for stopping, allocation among alternatives, and planning, including the overhead of metareasoning itself. These are foundations for Part I's learned allocation policy. Read for the objective and stopping problem; they do not require implementing a particular search heuristic in the harness. [Selecting Computations](https://aima.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/uai12-meta.pdf), [Learning to Select Computations](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.06892).
- EnCompass (NeurIPS 2025). Search over execution paths, separated from the agent workflow; ARC subset 4.3% to 11.7% at best-of-8 and 16.0% at best-of-36 (GPT-3.5), and a Reflexion agent 35.5% to 36.1% with best-first search at lower cost per task. Closest policy framing. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03571).
- APR — Learning Adaptive Parallel Reasoning with Language Models (Pan et al., 2025). Spawn and join are model actions trained end to end with RL. The parent chooses what context each child receives and continues from the joined results. The closest learned fork/join action protocol to Weft's, without executable state. Read alongside AsyncThink for action design and reward. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15466).
- Kimi K2.5 Agent Swarm (Kimi Team, 2026). A trainable orchestrator creates up to 100 subagents and is trained with parallel-agent RL. Two ideas transfer directly. "Critical steps" charge each parallel stage at its longest branch, which gives a latency-aware cost for the Pareto result. "Serial collapse" names the failure where an orchestrator with parallel capacity falls back to sequential work; watch for it in prompt-only runs and treat it as a training target. Its orchestrator is privileged, and its subagents do not inherit state. (Abstract and secondary write-ups; check the reward details in the report.) [Report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02276).
- WideSeek-R1 — Exploring Width Scaling for Broad Information Seeking via Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (2026). A lead agent and parallel subagents trained with multi-agent RL. Before training, results first improved and then declined as subagents were added, because the lead struggled with conflicting and redundant information. Evidence about where an untrained swarm's integration breaks, and consistent with prompting failure not gating training. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04634).
- Hogwild! Inference (Rodionov et al., 2025). Several instances of one model generate concurrently over a shared KV cache, see each other's progress, and coordinate without training. A prompt-only reference for copies of the same model collaborating. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06261).
- Collaborative Parallel Thinking — Share More, Search Less (Wang et al., EMNLP 2026). Parallel branches pool their discoveries at synchronization points instead of rediscovering them independently. Without training, this improves the accuracy-latency frontier on math tasks. Evidence for the value of the "what and when to communicate" decision in Part I. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27030).
- Second Thought — Reasoning in Parallel as LLM Agents Act and Observe (Sun et al., 2026). Forks auxiliary reasoning branches while the agent waits on a tool result and merges them when the observation arrives. Without training, it reports fewer turns and less main-thread decoding. Tool-wait time is idle model time that copies can fill. That is a latency lever separate from best-of-N, relevant to reaching the same latency at a lower $/token. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13667).
- Don't Build Multi-Agents (Yan, Cognition, 2025). The strongest practitioner argument against parallel subagents: delegation briefs lose context, and parallel workers make conflicting implicit decisions. It states the problem behind Part I's copy-versus-delegate distinction. A fork that inherits the full trace satisfies its context-sharing principle, and the conflicting-decisions concern becomes a communication and integration behavior the swarm can learn. [Post](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents).
- How we built our multi-agent research system (Hadfield et al., Anthropic, 2025). On BrowseComp, token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance, and the multi-agent system used about 15× the tokens of chat. Evidence that parallel workers turn additional tokens into results on breadth-heavy tasks, with a concrete cost multiplier. [Post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system).
- Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems (Kim et al., 2025). Controlled comparisons of agent architectures. Coordination helped on decomposable tasks and hurt on sequential planning, and returns diminished once a single agent was already strong. Relevant to the competence of staying serial when branching does not pay. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296).
- Scaling Long-Horizon LLM Agent via Context-Folding (Sun et al., 2025). Gives an agent native `branch` and `return(message)` actions to spin off a temporary sub-trajectory for a localized subtask and collapse the intermediate steps into a summary return, trained end to end with FoldGRPO. Reaches 58.0% Pass@1 on SWE-Bench Verified and 62.0% on BrowseComp-Plus at one-tenth the active context budget of ReAct (32K versus 327K). The single-stream analogue of using Weft's `fork` and `end(summary, artifacts)` for optional dynamic task-based compaction — with the advantage in Weft that delegating noisy exploration to a child fork keeps both transcripts strictly append-only and preserves KV-cache prefixes. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11967).
- Recursive Language Models (Zhang, Kraska, and Khattab, 2025). Keeps working state and sub-call outputs in the external execution environment rather than concatenating sub-agent transcripts into the root context, with the model invoking recursive LLM sub-calls and pulling only targeted slices into context. Post-training an 8B model (`RLM-Qwen3-8B`) for this recursive interface yields a 28.3% average gain on long-horizon tasks. Supports pull-by-reference integration (§3.1, §5) and small-student post-training for recursive organization (§9). [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24601).

14.3 Step 3 — evidence gathering, selection, models, tasks, and budgets (once the tool is available)

Read CodeMonkeys, CodeT, omp-best-of and its measured results, then LLM-as-a-Verifier, and inspect dsh-llm-as-a-verifier if its tool interface is useful. These readings inform what evidence the swarm can obtain and how it can decide among results; they do not assign the final decision to an external controller. Treat the small omp-best-of experiments as diagnostic examples, not a general accuracy estimate. Follow with Large Language Monkeys, Snell et al., and Agentless for sampling, compute allocation, and coding-task practice when choosing the first models, tasks, and budgets. Ordinary tasks that benefit from best-of-N provide the starting point; a special task-design campaign is not required.

- CodeMonkeys — Scaling Test-Time Compute for Software Engineering (Ehrlich et al., 2025). Combines serial code-and-test refinement with parallel candidate trajectories, then selects using generated tests and a dedicated selection trajectory. This is useful evidence about how multiple coding attempts become one delivered patch, including the work involved in testing and selection. Read its methods as possible behaviors the swarm can adopt and learn. Its fixed workflow need not become Weft's runtime policy, and its shared upfront work does not create a requirement for a separate setup-cost experiment. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723), [implementation](https://github.com/ScalingIntelligence/codemonkeys).
- CodeT — Code Generation with Generated Tests (Chen et al., 2022). Generates tests as well as code candidates and selects using execution agreement. It supplies a concrete way for agents to gather evidence about competing solutions when provided tests are incomplete. Generated tests are still fallible; their value is something to measure against withheld outcomes. For Weft, this suggests a learnable testing and comparison behavior available within the swarm. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397), [implementation](https://github.com/microsoft/CodeT).
- [omp-best-of](https://github.com/wolfiesch/omp-best-of) (wolfiesch, MIT). OMP extension: /best-of --n 2-8, isolated CoW workspaces, LLM-as-a-Verifier ranking, winner-only patch application. The closest implementation reference. Fixed N, user-invoked, no mid-task fork, no peer messaging. Its [measured results](https://github.com/wolfiesch/omp-best-of/blob/main/bench/RESULTS.md) are the cautionary tale: saturated pools, a verifier picking the failing candidate, and three evaluations tripling cost with no accuracy gain.
- LLM-as-a-Verifier (Kwok et al., 3.3k stars). Continuous scores from scoring-token logprob distributions, probabilistic pivot tournament; Terminal-Bench V2 86.5%, SWE-Bench Verified 78.2%. Selection method only; no harness, fork, or policy. dsh-llm-as-a-verifier exposes the same verifier as DeepSeek Harness tools: verify_compare, verify_select, verify_track. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05391).
- [Large Language Monkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787), [Snell et al. on scaling test-time compute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314), and [Agentless](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01489). Repeated sampling, compute-optimal test-time scaling, and sampling plus selection in coding practice.
- SpecBox — Speculative Sandbox Scheduling for Efficient LLM Agent Serving (Zhang et al., 2026). Prewarms sandboxes during LLM token generation and prefetches likely sandbox switches, trading speculative work for lower tail latency. Relevant to the serving-side latency argument in Part I: speculative parallel work can hold task latency while the hardware stays at an efficient batching point. It schedules sandboxes, not agent branches, and does not address live-workspace forking. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23933).
- Scaling Test-Time Compute for Agentic Coding (Kim et al., 2026). Converts each rollout into a compact structured summary, compares summaries by recursive tournament voting, and conditions new rollouts on distilled summaries. It reports Claude 4.5 Opus rising from 70.9% to 77.6% on SWE-bench Verified and from 46.9% to 59.1% on Terminal-Bench 2.0. Its summaries are close to Weft's emissions and peer-facing labels (§3.2). Comparing summaries is a behavior the swarm can use when reading every trace is too costly. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16529).
- SWE-Replay (Ding and Zhang, 2026). Branches from intermediate states of archived trajectories, restoring repository state from stored diffs, with a heuristic choosing branch points. It reports up to 17.4% lower cost on SWE-bench with resolve rates equal or higher. §5 keeps ended branches readable, so forking from an earlier point of a finished branch is an available swarm action. This is evidence that the action pays. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22129).
- Evaluating Test-Time Scaling of General LLM Agents (Li, Xiong, et al., 2026). Across search, coding, reasoning, and tool-use tasks, neither sequential nor parallel scaling reliably helped; the authors attribute the parallel shortfall to a verification gap. For general use without a golden checker, the swarm's own evidence gathering is the binding constraint. Also a candidate source of tasks for §9.2 transfer. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18998).
- The Limits of Inference Scaling Through Resampling (Stroebl, Kapoor, and Narayanan, 2024; first titled "Inference Scaling fLaws"). With imperfect verifiers such as incomplete unit tests, false positives cap what resampling can achieve. A reference for measuring swarm-generated evidence against withheld outcomes. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17501).
- Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization (Gao, Schulman, and Hilton, ICML 2023). Optimizing against a proxy reward model — by RL or by best-of-n selection — first raises then lowers gold reward as a function of distance from the initial policy, with a parametric inverted-U for best-of-n: more selection pressure against an imperfect scorer eventually selects for the scorer's errors. Weft's selector is a verifier in exactly this sense, and the hazard sharpens when the swarm both generates candidates and generates the tests that rank them (CodeT above): the same model family proposes the solutions and the evidence. Read alongside Stroebl et al. as the quantitative form of the verification gap: withheld-outcome accuracy of swarm-generated evidence is a measured quantity, not an assumption, and delivered accuracy should be reported against selection pressure (candidates considered, tests generated) rather than only against N. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760).
- When More Sampling Hurts — The Modal Ceiling and Correlation Ceiling of Test-Time Scaling (Bay and Yearick, 2026). Repeated samples from one model are correlated draws, not independent attempts: an effective number of samples, revealed by the sampling run itself, caps what majority vote or pass@k can extract, and beyond a few dozen draws coverage gains stop converting into selected-answer gains. Directly relevant to what "N" means in §7. Fixed-N independent attempts and inherited-context forks sit at different points on this correlation axis — a fork that shares the parent's full transcript and workspace shares their mistaken assumptions as well — so branch correlation is a property to instrument from the loom record (shared-prefix length, agreement among sibling emissions) rather than a reason to prefer one context mode in advance. The fresh-context mode (§4) and per-branch strategy hints are available decorrelators inside the existing interface; which of them the swarm uses, and whether they pay, is part of the behavior repertoire in step 4. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28661).
- Mode-Conditioning Unlocks Superior Test-Time Scaling (Wu, Goyal, and Raghunathan, 2025). Parallel sampling is limited by diversity collapse: the model concentrates on a few reasoning modes and repeated samples reproduce the same mistakes. Explicitly allocating compute across modes — via specialist models or mode-specific prefixes — restores scaling, matching standard sampling's Pass@1024 with a quarter of the samples in their controlled tasks. The mode-specific prefix is the transferable device: a fork tool call can carry a short strategy prefix for the child without turning the fork into a specialist delegation brief, which keeps Part I's copy-first design while giving branches a cheap way to not all try the same thing. Whether prompted copies diversify on their own or need the prefix is an empirical question for Stage 0 traces. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01127).
- S* — Test Time Scaling for Code Generation (Li et al., 2025). Combines parallel and sequential scaling, then generates inputs that distinguish candidates and selects by running the candidates on them. A concrete discriminating-test behavior the swarm can adopt. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14382).
- Risa — Disagree to Explore, Agree to Commit (Chen et al., 2026). Selects among parallel software-agent attempts by agreement in mixture-of-experts routing traces, without running tests. Evidence that model-internal signals can rank candidates where no checker exists. It applies only to open-weight MoE models, which fits local serving. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22191).
- AI Agents That Matter (Kapoor et al., 2024). Makes the case for cost-controlled agent evaluation, with accuracy plotted against dollar cost as a Pareto frontier, and documents reproducibility and holdout problems in agent benchmarks. Methodology for the main dollar-cost Pareto result. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502).
- SWE-Gym — Training Software Engineering Agents and Verifiers with SWE-Gym (Pan et al., ICML 2025). The first publicly available training environment for real-world SWE agents: 2,438 task instances from 11 Python repositories, each with a pre-installed executable environment and unit tests, used both to fine-tune agents and to train verifiers for inference-time scaling (up to +19 points resolve rate in their setups). Not Stage 0 material. For the training track (§9) it is the off-the-shelf alternative to building the first executable training-task pool from scratch, and its agent-plus-verifier pairing matches §9.1's control structure: the same environment supplies ordinary traces, loom traces, and a trained-selection diagnostic without conflating them. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21139), [implementation](https://github.com/SWE-Gym/SWE-Gym).
- SWE-smith — Scaling Data for Software Engineering Agents (Yang et al., 2025). A pipeline that synthesizes software-engineering tasks from any Python codebase by automatically breaking existing tests, yielding 50k instances from 128 repositories with executable environments. Relevant to §9.2 as an existing, validated instance of the controlled changed-requirement variant: a synthesized bug changes what the correct solution must do while the surrounding repository, tests, and validity check stay executable and checkable. Prefer adopting or extending this generator family over hand-rolling seeded transformations when the training track needs task variants at scale; record generator provenance and keep transformation families split as §9.2 already requires. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21798), [implementation](https://github.com/SWE-bench/SWE-smith).
- R2E-Gym — Procedural Environments and Hybrid Verifiers for Scaling Open-Weights SWE Agents (Jain et al., 2025). Procedurally curated executable environments (8k+ tasks) built from commits by test generation and back-translation, reducing reliance on human-written issues and tests, plus a systematic comparison of execution-based and execution-free verifiers whose strengths are complementary; combining them lifts a 32B open-weight model to 51% on SWE-bench Verified. Two transfers: for §9, a second source of executable training environments whose tasks are derived rather than collected; for §5 and §7, measured evidence that test execution and model judgment catch different failures, which is the shape of evidence a swarm can gather for itself — run what can be run, judge what cannot, and treat disagreement between the two as information rather than noise. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07164).

Serving economics for the $/token operating point in Part I and §7:

- SGLang (Zheng et al., 2023). A frontend `fork` primitive plus RadixAttention, which keeps KV cache for shared prefixes in a radix tree. This is the serving-side mechanism behind the KV reuse from branching: every child's fork-time prefix is already cached. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104).
- DeFT — Decoding with Flash Tree-Attention (Yao et al., ICLR 2025). An attention kernel for tree-structured decoding that avoids rereading shared-prefix KV and balances tree-shaped KV across partitions, with up to 2.2× end-to-end speedup. Relevant when a local server decodes many branches of one loom together. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00242).
- Autellix (Luo et al., 2025). Schedules an agent's LLM calls as one program using program-level statistics. It reports 4–15× higher program throughput than vLLM and SGLang at the same latency. The scheduling counterpart of the Part I hypothesis: run a loom's calls at an efficient batching point while holding task latency. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13965).
- Unlocking parallel test-time scaling for long-horizon agents (Bhat, Doubleword, 2026). Ran 64 concurrent mini-swe-agent instances on DeepSeek-V4-Pro against all 731 public SWE-bench Pro problems on one 8×B300 node: 46,784 attempts in 24 hours, at about $0.05 each. A single attempt averaged 51.2%. Accepting any of the 64 reached 70.7%, which is an oracle bound rather than a selected result. A measured dollars-per-attempt reference for a throughput-oriented serving point. (Page partly truncated when read.) [Post](https://blog.doubleword.ai/swe-bench-pro-64-deepseek-agents).

14.4 Step 4 — prompt learning and behaviors the swarm can discover

Read GEPA and the project's plan-doc-eval before building the prompt-search loop. Give the optimizer complete outcome and cost records, including the swarm's own verification, communication, and integration work; the learning target is the whole organization policy. The rest of this step is a repertoire of revision, sampling, exploration, and evaluation strategies, plus orchestration examples to consult when a concrete interface question arises. These are sources of candidate behavior and implementation ideas; the swarm remains responsible for deciding which behavior to use.

- GEPA — Reflective Prompt Evolution Can Outperform Reinforcement Learning (Agrawal et al., 2025). A close methodological match to Stage 0: reflect on execution trajectories, propose prompt changes, and retain complementary candidates through Pareto-based evolutionary search. Inspect the implementation before writing a custom genetic optimizer. The proposed application is to evolve the swarm's instructions for organization, including selection and integration, against delivered outcomes and costs. Compare its evaluation and population management with the existing plan-doc-eval discipline. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457), [implementation](https://github.com/gepa-ai/gepa).
- [Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366), [self-consistency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171), [Tree of Thoughts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601), and [LATS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04406). The strategy space the model may already know: revision, sampling, exploration, and evaluation.
- SWE-Search — Enhancing Software Agents with Monte Carlo Tree Search and Iterative Refinement (Antoniades et al., 2024). Combines repository-level agent execution with MCTS, numerical and qualitative evaluation, and feedback among agent roles. Read for useful exploration and evaluation patterns, along with their costs. It provides a concrete reference for investigating alternatives during execution; adopting its fixed search controller or role decomposition is not a requirement. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20285).
- Hail Hydra (MIT). Task-level speculative execution: cheap models draft, the orchestrator verifies or redoes. Task delegation, not session forking; a reference for speculative delegation.
- agentbox, uzi, pro-workflow, Trail (operation database for worktrees), and Grove (multiple agents on git worktrees). User-orchestrated parallel coding agents in git worktrees, with inbox or board UIs. The manual version of the product, and orchestration examples for concrete interface questions.
- ATLAS — Agentic Test-time Learning-to-Allocate Scaling (Qin, Cao, and Xie, 2026). A prompted orchestrator decides when to dispatch another solver, when to stop, and how to synthesize. On HLE, LiveCodeBench, and GPQA it uses fewer calls than fixed workflows. Evidence that current models can make allocation and stopping decisions from a prompt. Its solvers start fresh, so it tests allocation without inherited state. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01667).
- AB-MCTS — Wider or Deeper? Scaling LLM Inference-Time Compute with Adaptive Branching Tree Search (Inoue et al., Sakana AI, 2025). Adaptively chooses between generating a new attempt and refining an existing one. Weft faces the same choice as forking versus continuing. Read it as part of the behavior repertoire, not as a controller to adopt. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04412).
- ACE — Agentic Context Engineering (Zhang et al., 2025). Evolves an agent's instructions as a playbook through incremental, curated updates. This avoids the collapse that comes from repeatedly rewriting a prompt wholesale. Complements GEPA for evolving the swarm's organization instructions across many runs. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618).
- Darwin Gödel Machine (Zhang et al., 2025). Open-ended, archive-based evolution of a coding agent's own harness, evaluated on SWE-bench and Polyglot. A reference for population and archive management in harness search, and for the evidence a later self-improvement claim (§9) would need. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954).

14.5 Step 5 — learning from traces and removing scaffolds (when preparing training)

Read Learning by Distilling Context, then STaR, and return to AsyncThink's training procedure. They bear on internalizing prompting, learning from generated traces, and training organization itself. Read Quiet-STaR afterward as an adjacent approach to learning useful reasoning. Use the matched teacher-source controls in §9.1 to interpret gains.

- [Learning by Distilling Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15189), [STaR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465), and [Quiet-STaR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09629). The elicitation-to-no-scaffold training move (§9.4) and its collapse risk.
- Tree-GRPO — Tree Search for LLM Agent Reinforcement Learning (Ji et al., ICLR 2026) and TreePO — Bridging the Gap of Policy Optimization and Efficacy and Inference Efficiency with Heuristic Tree-based Modeling (Li et al., 2025). Multi-turn agent RL over tree-structured rollouts that share action-observation prefixes. Sibling branches forked from the same node form an intra-tree comparison group that yields step-level process supervision from sparse outcome rewards alone (provably equivalent to step-level preference learning at the branch point) while amortizing rollout compute across shared prefixes. Directly applicable to §9: recorded looms are already shared-prefix trees with outcome and cost labels, providing fork-point credit assignment without retrospective teacher rollbacks. Pair with FoldGRPO (Context-Folding, §14.2) when rewarding optional task-based context compaction. [Tree-GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21240), [TreePO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17445).
- TraceGraph (Nian et al., 2026). Builds a graph over action-observation states from pooled rollouts, marks "trap" regions, and evaluates continuations from the same prefix. A method for reading the loom record across tasks: where forks paid off, where branches shared a mistaken assumption, and which decisions to select for training. (Abstract only.) [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31308).
- MAST — Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? (Cemri et al., 2025). A taxonomy of multi-agent failures covering specification, inter-agent misalignment, and verification. A starting vocabulary for the §7 failure diagnoses and for labeling failed looms. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657).

14.6 Step 6 — VM state, distributed deployment, and broader architectures (later versions)

Read [smolvm's branching documentation](https://github.com/smol-machines/smolvm/blob/main/docs/branching.md), [Cloud Hypervisor's snapshot/restore documentation](https://github.com/cloud-hypervisor/cloud-hypervisor/blob/main/docs/snapshot_restore.md), Firecracker, and Waypoint/CRIU when extending the fork to live-process and memory state. Use gVisor, E2B, microsandbox, and SnowFlock as targeted comparisons. Distinguish shared disk extents from shared resident memory and verify how the source continues after a fork; the conformance recipe is in §11, the implementation catalog and the scale model are below, and revisiting the catalog is only needed when a chosen backend exposes a concrete limitation. Return to the minimal-sufficient-inference review, Omerta, and SnowFlock when placement, state transfer, or communication becomes a measured constraint. Keep Petals, compound AI systems, Mixture-of-Agents, FrugalGPT, AlphaCode, distributed training, MoE, and hardware scaling as later background.

- smolvm (smol-machines, Apache-2.0, 7k stars). Branchable microVMs: live CoW branches with processes, memory and disk, recursive sharing, disk-chain compaction, warm pools. The best full-session fork primitive; a deferred backend.
- Morph Cloud Infinibranch. A commercial VM snapshot, branch, and restore service for agent workloads, quoted at under 250 ms per operation. A rental reference for live-process branching if the local VM path stalls. [Developer page](https://cloud.morph.so/developers).
- forkd (jrimmer). A KVM microVM fork tool. It claims 100 children in about 100 ms from a warm parent and live-VM branching in about 150 ms. A step 6 candidate to test against the §11 conformance recipe. (README only.) [Repository](https://github.com/jrimmer/forkd).

VM implementations, classified by whether a fork of a fork layers on the immediate parent or re-bases on the child (moved from §11):
- Cloud Hypervisor (layers): memory_restore_mode=copyonwrite maps the parent snapshot COW; snapshotting a VM restored that way copies the restore source with a reflink and writes only pages changed since the restore, a new layer relative to the immediate parent, recursively. Snapshot and restore source must stay on the same reflink-capable filesystem and the restore source must remain unchanged.
- Firecracker (layers, manual): diff snapshots are layers relative to the last snapshot of the same VM; snapshot-editor rebase merges them. A fork of a fork can be a diff layer on the child, but diff snapshots are developer preview and chain management is manual; verify resumability of the chain.
- SnowFlock (layers, research): lazy state replication gives each child a COW view of their parent, and children of children share the child's state. The classic recursive design, cross-host.
- microsandbox (verify): msb fork / fork_many with --cow-mem; per-child memory sharing and a reusable local memory cache are documented, but not whether a fork of a fork layers on the child or re-bases by snapshotting the child. Test before relying on it.
- E2B (re-bases): fork snapshots the source and boots N children; a fork of a fork likely snapshots the child, materializing its private pages, rather than adding a transitive layer. Verify if chain semantics matter.
- Firecracker full snapshots, PandaStack, Daytona hot snapshots, Apple VZ save/restore, Arrakis: snapshot/restore primitives, each restore relative to a stored image rather than a chain of live layers; fast fork from one checkpoint, not recursive COW.
- Apple Virtualization.framework: saveMachineStateTo / restoreMachineStateFrom (macOS 14+) for a paused VM; no documented COW sharing across restores. Relevant to Omerta's Swift nodes, not a high-fan-out fork primitive.
- Modal: filesystem snapshots only; no memory fork.
- gVisor, CRIU / Kubernetes: checkpoint/restore with memory copy; no COW layers. gVisor has background restore and zero-page exclusion; Kubernetes container checkpoint is beta since 1.30.
- agent-sandbox: Kubernetes Sandbox CRD, pause/resume and warm pools; orchestrator only, no fork.
- Blaxel: microVM standby resume under 25 ms; no fork documented.

Scale model for thousands of VM branches (moved from §11.1):
- Per-branch cost for a small microVM: shared pages counted once across the tree, read-only; unique memory, the pages the branch writes, as the dominant marginal cost; per-VM overhead of kernel and device state, EPT/NPT (single-digit MB for a few GB of guest RAM), vCPU thread(s), network namespace or TAP, file descriptors, and snapshot-layer metadata; snapshot storage of one base plus per-branch diff layers, compacted periodically.
- Requirements at 1,000 branches: same-host placement for COW locality, with cross-host forks (which ship memory and disk) for capacity only and only emissions crossing hosts; a recursive tree rather than a flat fan-out, caching a snapshot when several children fork from one point; parking and eviction of idle branches, since most are idle at any moment; admission control and accounting with per-branch caps, shared-page accounting, and deferral when the host budget is exhausted; hierarchical loom_status summarized per subtree with full transcripts in the log, never in context; synthetic emissions for crashes and timeouts, orphan reparenting, and retries, because failures are normal at that scale; per-branch network isolation, encrypted snapshots, and verified cleanup; a multi-host scheduler that places a fork near its parent and treats cross-host placement as a migration.
- Cost-Effective Parallel Computing (Wood and Hill, IEEE Computer, 1995). Parallelism pays whenever speedup exceeds costup, the ratio of the parallel system's cost to a uniprocessor's; because memory dominates system cost, 8-, 16-, and 32-processor systems beat a uniprocessor at speedups of about 3.3, 5.0, and 8.6 on 1994 prices. The hardware-cost argument behind Part I: added processing is cheap relative to the rest of the machine, so sublinear parallel efficiency is still economical. [Record](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/4d0dc7f5-183a-4380-8c8d-4b04c6c69ebe).
- A Case for NOW (Networks of Workstations) (Anderson, Culler, and Patterson, IEEE Micro, 1995). Argues that clusters of commodity workstations, not massively parallel machines, would become the main platform for science and engineering, on price-performance and on pooled memory and disks; names communication, multi-OS coordination, and enterprise file systems as the open problems and reports a 100-node prototype. The commodity-over-specialized argument that Part I's hardware paragraph reuses. [Paper](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~tom/pubs/caseNOW.html).
- BEOWULF: A Parallel Workstation for Scientific Computation (Becker, Sterling, Savarese, Dorband, Ranawake, and Packer, ICPP, 1995). Sixteen PC processing modules over multiple Ethernets at NASA Goddard, built to mimic a supercomputer from commodity hardware; the pattern the Top500 then followed. [Paper](https://webhome.phy.duke.edu/~rgb/brahma/Resources/beowulf/papers/ICPP95/icpp95.html).
