Weft — Forking the computer

Project handoff

Part I — Introduction: copies are the native affordance

Humans have a single continuing existence and cannot trivially copy themselves into simultaneous continuations. That pattern casts a shadow over how we design agents. A digital agent, by contrast, can copy their working state and pursue several continuations at once. The resulting tree of existences is beyond human comprehension, much like the models themselves. But much existing work forebodes much better performance and lower cost. Best-of-N evaluations show increased performance, and fork-summarize-and-join models decrease cost, but these systems are overly constrained by a desire for control and full understanding. No work has delivered a fully transparent and trainable loom for arbitrary graphs of machine states. This project takes the bitter lesson instead: learn the organization of the tree rather than prescribe it.

The key primitive this project uses is a fork of the full machine+agent state, the session and the executable workspace branch together, available to the models as a tool call. Instead of the prescribed parallelism used today, we build a loom that includes the state of the computer itself. A copy can act on the files, artifacts, and tool results accumulated so far without reconstructing the situation from a description. Taking the bitter lesson seriously means learning the organization policy from outcomes. Internally, the swarm determines when to fork, where, to which model or specialization, how wide, how much context to inherit, what each branch should do, what and when to communicate, what evidence to use, which results to incorporate, and when to stop. The swarm owns selection, integration, and final delivery, and chooses whether to interrupt a peer or consult a quarantined copy of the peer (§5). Since these decisions are models', the paradigm grows more capable and efficient as the models do.

Relative to training agents to generate and run massively parallel tasks themselves, forking lowers the burden of asking for help. Invoking a specialized subagent or predefined workflow ordinarily asks the agent to recognize both that they need help and what kind of help to request. A fork can require only the judgement that another continuation could be useful. The copy inherits the situation and can discover what work is needed. A detailed task decomposition or specialist brief is optional. This also preserves information whose importance the parent has not yet recognized and therefore might omit from a delegation brief.

The economic case rests on established results. The rise of Mixture of Experts (MoE) models has shown that frontier capabilities do not depend on all the weights operating continuously and with dense connection. The success of subagents and summaries has shown that the average context can stay small without hurting performance. Managed best-of-N has shown higher performance at lower cost and that parallel work shortens a task's latency while maintaining its performance. Lower task latency allows running inference servers at more profitable operating points without hurting end users' project timelines. These are all examples of exploiting the tree of machine states for higher performance and lower cost, but they do not make that tree trainable or transparent.

Since the techniques are not transparent to the user, deployed agents do not make full use of them, so their realized economic value is limited. Each is a prescription: someone decides in advance when to branch, what to delegate, and how to combine the results. Improving a prescription takes specialized training; gains in the base model's general intelligence only reach it partially. Almost any task can benefit from forking, and deciding to fork only requires a judgement on uncertainty, so it is generic across tasks. Generic means trainable without special treatment.

And the trainability extends to follow-up architectures likely to increase model performance beyond the frontier. The same primitive extends from identical copies to transparent model specialization (§12.1): one base model controls the root session while forks can target models differentiated by prompting or weights. This allows capturing the benefit of specialization demonstrated by MoE. Token-level MoE routes every token across experts and requires tight, high-bandwidth interconnects; routing at `fork` shifts communication to branch boundaries and compact emissions, so specialized models on separate modest machines can do substantial local work over the inherited session and workspace before reporting back, without prescribed specialist roles or lossy delegation briefs.

The bitter lesson bears on hardware as well. The most capable models are being built to target hardware that is extremely expensive and not widely available: large nodes with tight interconnects. If scaling is the priority, the target cannot be one machine scaled up indefinitely. It has to be models small enough to run on devices that are distributed geographically and in every other way devices can be distributed, which means consumer-level hardware in very large swarms. The same argument was made in systems research when large shared-memory multiprocessors were the frontier: on economics alone, clusters of commodity machines won, and high-performance computing then built its supercomputers from commodity parts (§14.6). Note that none of this implies that the collapse in the cost of programming leaves consumer hardware untouched: the shape of that compute is likely to change now that humans are not the ones programming it (§13).
